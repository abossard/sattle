# Ch. 10 Sources and adapters

**Question.** How do records from Azure, Kubernetes and the network observer become inputs of the engine, and what may be concluded from what a source says and from what it does not say?

**What you can state at the end.** An adapter turns the records of one source into typed rows and does nothing else; names and graph facts are made later, by the rules. A record delivered twice changes nothing. A source withdraws only its own contribution, so an element stays while another source supports it. Absence from a scan means deletion only where the scan declares that it saw everything in its scope. Loss of data is declared and never read as deletion. Under the documented contract of Kubernetes, a list followed by a watch gives the true state of a collection. A recording of the inputs gives the same versions every time it is processed. A live run is reproduced by processing its recording again, and a live run that is killed and restarted ends in the state of a run that was never killed; both hold under named premises.

This chapter uses the facts about sources (Ch. 0, 0.4), identity (Ch. 1), the four clocks (Ch. 3, 3.4), rows, ingestion projections and `IdOf` (Ch. 5), measurements and closed windows (Ch. 7), and support and withdrawal (Ch. 9).

**Running example.** The shop runs in one cluster. Kubernetes lists its pods and then reports every change. The network observer reports the calls between the pods and, under load, says that it has lost some. The resource list of Azure is read in pages. Pod `api-7c9` is deleted while the network observer still has a call from it in its buffer.

## 10.1 Definitions

**Definition 10.1 (adapter).** An *adapter* is a part of the program that reads the native records of one source and produces **rows** of a declared shape (a `RowType`, Ch. 2, Def. 2.2), together with declarations of coverage (Def. 10.4). It hides the formats and versions of the source. It does not resolve identity and creates no graph facts. Each adapter declares the digest of the release of the row shape it produces, and is registered under a name that a `Source` of a space refers to (Def. 5.8). A new adapter needs a new release of the program.

**Definition 10.2 (envelope, delivery key).** A record that is captured or sent on is wrapped in an *envelope* `{v, source, capturedAt, seq, key, meta, payload}`.
- `payload` is the native record, unchanged.
- `meta` is the context of the capture: the query, the page, the token for resuming.
- `capturedAt` is the time on the clock of the capturing program when it received the record. It is *not* the time of the event: that is inside the payload and becomes the observed time `τ` (Def. 7.2).
- `seq` orders the records of one file only.
- The **delivery key** `key = <source>/<natural id>` is stable: the same record delivered twice has the same key. Where the source gives no natural id, the key is a hash of the payload with a secret added (a *salt*).

**Definition 10.3 (observation, contribution, withdrawal).** An *observation* is a row of an adapter with its source, delivery key and position. A *contribution* is the set of fragments (Def. 9.7) that one record of a source causes, named by a stable **contribution key**. A source can replace or withdraw its contribution without deleting other support for the same graph fact.

**Definition 10.4 (coverage, authoritative absence).** A *coverage declaration* `Coverage(scope, level, scan)` is made by an adapter: the scan `scan` claims to have listed every record of `scope` at the level of consistency `level`. **Authoritative absence** of an element in the scope is the conclusion, from such a declaration, that what the scan did not list does not exist. Without a declaration, absence from a result means *unknown*, not deletion. A *reconciliation policy* is a rule `reconcile(source, scope)` that withdraws the contribution of every element of `scope` that a scan did not return, provided the scan's declared level is at least the level `ℓ` that the policy requires.

*Example:* a Kubernetes list of the pods of namespace `store` declares that it holds every pod of that namespace as of one version. A pod that the engine knows and the list does not hold is deleted. A page of the Azure resource list that was cut short declares nothing, so a resource missing from it stays.

**Definition 10.5 (ingestion pipeline).** There is one pipeline, in this order. The adapter turns native records into rows. The **ingestion projection** (purpose `ingestion`, Def. 5.1) resolves identity (`IdOf` over fields of the row, Def. 5.9; *allocate* or a lookup, Def. 1.9 and 1.10) and emits the facts of the master graph. The ingestion projection is required.

**Definition 10.6 (structure rows and telemetry rows).** A field of a row shape is graph data or telemetry according to the property it feeds. Observations about topology and state (resources, objects, events) feed the structural plane. Counters, sums of flows and other measurements feed the telemetry plane as samples `(τ, w, x, i)` (Def. 7.2). A flow is a sample on a relationship, never a relationship (Ch. 11, assumption A1).

Rules per source:
- **Network observer.** When only names are given, the identity of an endpoint is scoped by cluster and namespace. Reuse of an address is not evidence of identity. A `lost_events` message states incompleteness (`Partial(sourceLoss)` on the windows it touches, Def. 7.7) and is never read as a deletion.
- **Azure resource list.** A resource that a query returns is an observation. Having read all pages is not a consistent picture of one moment. Deletion by absence needs a declared reconciliation policy, because filters in the query, partial scopes and late indexing rule out an unconditional rule.
- **Kubernetes.** Warning events are observations about objects, not changes of state. The `resourceVersion` of Kubernetes is data of the source, used for equality and for resuming a watch. The engine does not rely on its order: the current documentation of the Kubernetes interface promises versions that can be ordered as integers only from release 1.35 on ([k8s-api-concepts]), and the cluster of the recordings ran 1.34. A pod is identified by its uid within its cluster; a deployment uses another identity domain (Ch. 5). `DELETED` withdraws the source's contribution while another source may still support the entity. An expired watch needs a new list.
- **Network logs of Azure.** The layout of a tuple is read by its format version, never by one parser of fixed positions. The older log format is read only if a space binds it explicitly, and it retires on 30 September 2027 (Fact 0.4).
- **OpenTelemetry.** A set of attributes gives context for identity, not an identity that all sources share (Fact 0.5). The id of a running instance is kept apart from the name of the service. Measurements are read by their time behavior: amounts per period and running totals map to the time behavior and combine rule of the property they feed (Def. 2.11).

## 10.3 Theorems

**Theorem 10.1 (delivering twice changes nothing).** Delivering a record again with the same delivery key changes neither the graph nor the support of any element. An observation with a new delivery key is processed: it replaces the contribution with the same contribution key, and adds one otherwise.

*Proof.* Contributions are keyed: replacing the contribution of key `c` by the same fragments leaves the support `σ` of Def. 9.7 unchanged, because `σ` is the sum over keyed contributions, one term per key. A batch of delivery is not a unit of success or failure and carries no meaning (Theorem 6.2(a)). Samples of telemetry are kept once per `(k, p, τ, i)` (Def. 7.2). ∎

**Theorem 10.2 (withdrawal removes one source's support).** A withdrawal by source `s` removes exactly the contribution of `s` for the withdrawn record. The element remains iff its support from the other contributions is positive.

*Proof.* Def. 9.7 and Theorem 9.5. ∎

*Example:* Kubernetes reports `DELETED` for pod `api-7c9`. Its contribution is withdrawn. If the network observer's call still names the pod and a rule creates the pod from flows too, the pod stays with that support; otherwise it goes.

**Theorem 10.3 (soundness of reconciliation).** Let a scan `scan` satisfy its coverage declaration `Coverage(scope, level, scan)`: it did list every record of `scope` at that level. Let the engine have processed nothing from that source about the scope that is newer than the scan's snapshot, neither a contribution nor a deletion. Then the withdrawals of `reconcile` are exactly the elements the engine holds from that source that were deleted at the source, at the scan's level of consistency. No reconcile rule may fire without a coverage declaration with `level ≥ ℓ`.

The theorem is proved relative to the declaration. Whether the scan of a given source meets its declaration is a fact about that source, and each adapter must be checked against it. The recordings show what the sources give for it: pages of the Azure resource list report `$skipToken` and `resultTruncated`, and a Kubernetes list gives a consistent picture at one version.

*Why the second hypothesis is needed.* A list at one version returns `{a}`. The same source reports the creation of `b` at a later version, and the engine processes that record before it applies the list. Then `b` is not in the list and would be withdrawn although it exists. With the order of Theorem 10.5, list first and watch after, the hypothesis holds, because the watch starts after the list.

*Proof.* By Def. 10.4 an element of `scope` that is absent from a complete scan is absent from the source at that level, and by the second hypothesis the engine has processed nothing newer about it. Withdrawing its contribution is the definition of deletion for a source that has no explicit record of deletion. Elements outside `scope`, and elements of a scan without a declaration, are not touched. ∎

**Theorem 10.4 (loss is not deletion).** No `lost_events`, page cut short, query refused for load, expired watch or failed read causes a withdrawal. It marks coverage as incomplete (a `Partial` closed window for telemetry, an outcome `Unavailable` or `MoreAvailable` for reads, Def. 11.7) or causes a new list.

*Proof.* A withdrawal is produced only by an explicit record of deletion (Theorem 10.2), by a reconcile rule (Theorem 10.3), and by the expiry of a `Retain` (Def. 5.15), which depends on logical time only. None of them fires on loss. ∎

*Example:* the network observer says it lost events on one machine at 10:58. The window of calls that contains 10:58 is marked `Partial(sourceLoss)`. The call from `api-7c9` that was lost is not a deletion of anything.

**Theorem 10.5 (list then watch, resumption).** Assume the premises KW1 to KW3 on the Kubernetes interface. Let a list of a collection return the set of objects `O₀` and the version `v₀`, and let a watch started at `v₀` have delivered the events `ε₁ … ε_n` without a 410 answer. Let `O_j` be `O_{j−1}` with `ε_j` applied: `ADDED` and `MODIFIED` put the object under its uid, `DELETED` removes it, `BOOKMARK` changes nothing. Then `O_n` is the state of the collection after the operation that `ε_n` reports. If the watch ends with 410 Gone, a new list gives the state at its own version, and the statement applies again from there.

**Premises (the contract of list and watch, [k8s-api-concepts], section "Efficient detection of changes", read on 2026-11-11).**
- **KW1.** A list returns the objects of the collection as of one `resourceVersion`, and returns that version.
- **KW2.** A watch started at a version delivers one event for every create, update and delete on the collection after that version, with the outcome of the operation, in the order of the operations. The documentation says that the stream lists the outcome of the operations after the given version, so that a client can follow changes "without missing any events". That the events arrive in the order of the operations is this paper's reading of "stream of changes", and the theorem needs it.
- **KW3.** A server keeps the history of changes for a limited time. A watch that asks for an older version fails with 410 Gone, and the client must list again and watch from the version the list returns.

The premises are properties of an outside system, taken from its documentation. They cannot be tested from the project, and the theorem is as strong as that documentation. The recordings follow this protocol.

*Proof.* By induction on `n`. `O₀` is the state at `v₀` (KW1). Let `O_{j−1}` be the state after the operation of `ε_{j−1}`, or at `v₀` for `j = 1`. By KW2 the next operation on the collection is the one that `ε_j` reports, and the event carries its outcome: after a create or an update the object is as the event gives it, after a delete it is gone. Applying `ε_j` to `O_{j−1}` therefore gives the state after that operation. A `BOOKMARK` reports no operation. After a 410, KW3 and KW1 give a new base. ∎

*In the engine.* An `ADDED` or `MODIFIED` event replaces the contribution of the object under its contribution key (kind and uid), and a `DELETED` event withdraws it (Theorems 10.1 and 10.2). The delivery key, which includes the version, only drops repeats. A new list is a coverage declaration for its kind (Theorem 10.3): what the engine knows of the kind and the list does not mention is withdrawn (10.6, markers of the forwarder).

**Theorem 10.6 (processing a recording again gives the same versions).** Order the records of a capture (10.5) by `(capturedAt, file path, seq)`, and give each record the ingestion sequence number of its position; the recording of a live run (Def. 10.7) is one file and is processed in its line order. Then, with pinned definitions and the same settings (batch size, step of logical time, late horizon), processing gives the same log of versions on every run.

*Proof.* The sort key is a total order on the records of a recording: files written by several programs carry separate sequences, so `capturedAt` and the file path break ties between files, and `seq` orders within a file. The resulting sequence is a recorded input sequence, and Theorem 6.3 applies. ∎

The order of a recording is the order in which the capturing programs received the records. It is not the order in which the sources emitted them. The engine does not promise equal results for different interleavings of independent sources. A benchmark of Ch. 13 processes recordings at 1, 10 and 100 times their speed by shrinking the gaps in `capturedAt`, never by reordering.

## 10.4 Identity of observed endpoints

- A flow names its endpoints by namespace and pod name, plus identities that the network observer assigns. The key `podName(cluster, namespace, name)` is linked to the pod's uid and bound for good (Ch. 1, Def. 1.10). When a pod with a stable name is created again under a new uid, the link from its Kubernetes row therefore merges the new pod with the old one, the smaller id surviving (Theorem 1.7), and flows that name it go to that one entity. This is a limit: to keep the two pods apart, a name would have to be resolved through graph data that change with the log, not used as an identity key, or the name key would have to carry the incarnation of the pod.
- An endpoint known only by its address is **scoped to the observation**: it is keyed by cluster, address and a lease bounded in time, or marked as scoped to the observation, because an address is reused and is not an identity that lasts.
- Rows from several adapters denote one entity iff they use the same identity domain and equal canonical components (Theorem 1.4), or through an accepted equivalence (`Link`, Ch. 5). The Kubernetes row of a pod uses the uid, and links uid and pod name with confidence 1 (Ch. 5, Def. 5.9). What a reused name does to that link has not been measured: no recording holds a pod with a stable name that was created again.

## 10.5 Recordings

- A *recording* (a capture) is a set of files, one per source, with one envelope per line. Recordings are not kept in the project's repository, because they hold real names. A *fixture* is a sample of a recording with the same envelopes, in which every real name is replaced; a program that builds the fixtures checks that no real name is left. The replacement is a function of the value and a secret, so the same real value gets the same replacement in every source, and references still join.
- The recordings were taken from a test environment, a small demo workload in a real cluster with the forwarding set up by the project. It is not the estate of Ch. 11.
- **The cluster of the recordings no longer exists.** Its group of resources was deleted on 2026-11-10, not by this project, as runs of the project that the authors did not repeat report (Ch. 14, 14.1). So no further real recording, no new setup of the test environment and no run against Event Hubs is possible until a cluster exists. The newest recording has records from every source.
- Limits of the recordings: the network logs of Azure could not be read from outside the cluster under the owner's security policy, and were read through a short-lived pod inside it. The history of the network observer is bounded by its buffer (the capture takes the last 3,000 flows per machine and then follows). Event Hubs at the size used delivers about 125 events per second (10.6), so only the demo namespaces are forwarded. No `lost_events` occurred in the recordings.

## 10.6 Network logs, events and the live stream

**Network logs of Azure as a source.** The tuples of one record become rows of the shape `FlowLogRow`, one row per tuple. One `readonly` rule of the shop's ingestion projection keeps the tuples with state `D` (denied) or `B` (begun), puts them into windows of 60 s by the time of the tuple with the late policy `revise`, and updates the virtual network resource with the telemetry properties `deniedFlows` and `newFlows`. The health signal "denied flows" of Ch. 7 (7.4) reads the first. The rule creates no endpoint entities: the addresses and the states `C` (continuing) and `E` (ended) are fields that no rule uses, and tuples are not joined into connections. The time of a tuple is the time of the event (observed time `τ`, Def. 7.2); the time of a record is when the platform wrote it. Observed time therefore does not increase along the stream: tuples inside a record are not sorted and a later record can carry older tuples, so rows arrive late for their windows. Reading a file again returns the same records, so a record is applied once only through its delivery key (Theorem 10.1); the capture keys a record by the path of the file and the time of the record.

*Measured* on the 95-minute recording: 522 records with 175,265 tuples, of which 85,491 `B`, 85,476 `E`, 4,170 `C` and 128 `D`, from 10:19:47 to 12:30:48 (UTC), and 175,265 rows consumed with none rejected. The authors counted the states from the recording. Identity of endpoints known only by address (10.4) is therefore **not exercised**: no rule uses the addresses.

**Events as evidence elements.** Two adapters read the diagnostic records that Azure sends to Event Hubs: one turns records of the log of management actions into rows `ActivityRow`, the other turns completed requests of the Kubernetes audit log into rows `AuditRow`. Each event is one element and not only a count (Ch. 9, 9.6). The counts stay as telemetry as well. Tests: a started and a succeeded record give two events with one `Concerns` target whatever the case of the resource id; only completed audit requests are kept, and no renewals of leases; of two Kubernetes events only the warning becomes an element.

The other 532 and 603 are the resources, pods, services, machines and deployments (456 and 491) and the meters (76 and 112). Evidence elements therefore make up most of the master graph on both recordings.

**One event, one identity.** An event that arrives through both Azure routes, the interface for past actions and the diagnostic records, is one element: both adapters name it by the same key (correlation, operation, resource, status, event time). Two limits remain, reported by the engine's developers and not checked by the authors. 231 events of the newest recording still get two elements, because the two routes give times that differ by 1 to 100 ms and an identity key cannot express a tolerance. And 55 events between a raw count of the recording and the engine's count are not explained.

**Definition 10.7 (live input).** A *live input* reads a stream of Event Hubs whose events are envelopes (Def. 10.2). It uses the same adapters as the processing of a recording. Its logical time advances from the recorded `capturedAt` of the events, never from the clock on the wall, so it is an input of the same kind as an advance `Advance(λ)` of a recording. The reader remembers its position by delivery key and sequence number, drops duplicates by key, and writes every consumed envelope to a file, the *recording of the run*. Processing that file again is meant to give the digest of the state of the run.

**Proposition 10.7 (a live run is reproduced by its recording).** Let a live run consume the envelopes `e_1 … e_N`. Under the premises LV1 to LV4, processing its recording with the same definitions ends in the state digest of the live run.
- **LV1 (the recording is the consumed sequence).** The recording holds exactly `e_1 … e_N`, each once, in the order of consumption.
- **LV2 (an envelope gives the same inputs in both runs).** The rows and the advances of logical time that an envelope causes are a function of the envelope, the envelopes before it and the settings of the run (how often time advances, how late a row may be): an adapter is a function of the record (Def. 10.1), and logical time advances from the recorded `capturedAt` (Def. 10.7), never from the clock on the wall.
- **LV3 (same settings).** The second processing uses the late horizon and the step of logical time of the live run.
- **LV4.** The premises B1 to B4 of Theorem 6.2 hold for the definitions and the input.

*Proof.* By LV1 and LV2 the live run and the second processing evaluate the same sequence of inputs: the same rows and advances, in the same order, with the same ingestion sequence numbers. They differ only in where the sequence is cut into steps: the live run writes out its pending rows at moments that depend on timing, and the second processing cuts by batch size. By LV3 the test whether a row is late is, in both runs, the same function of the row and of the frontier at its position (premise B2). By Theorem 6.2(b), which needs LV4, the state after the last input is the same for every cut. The state digest is a function of the state (Theorem 3.3). ∎

The two logs of commits can differ, because a step loses the boundaries of versions inside it (Theorem 6.2(b)). The proposition is about the state digest.

The proof has been read, not checked by a program. LV1 and LV2 are properties of the reader and of the adapters, LV3 of how the second processing is started. They are tested on examples against a stand-in or a fake source, never on Event Hubs, and the reproduction test runs a loop of its own with the same steps, not the prototype's reader of the stream.
- *Reproduction.* A test writes the envelopes of the fixtures in a shuffled order of arrival (every line moved up to 0, 6 or 40 places from its position by capture time, three seeds), processes them one by one with the late horizon of a live run (3 windows), writes out the pending rows at random moments, and then processes the recording in file order with the same horizon. The state digests are equal in the three cases. A second test checks that the recording is read in file order and not in order of capture time. A third does the same on the newest real recording (distance 25, one seed).
- *Order and kills.* For each envelope the reader goes through four steps in this order: receive, write to the *journal* (with the position in the source), write to the recording, process. The journal is the truth and is written first; the recording never holds an envelope the journal lacks; a start that finds the recording shorter than the journal appends the missing envelopes before it reads the source. A test stops a run at each of the four steps of the envelopes with index 2, 25 and 60 of 80 shuffled envelopes of the synthetic estate (by an exception raised from inside the program, on the store in memory) and starts it again. The digest equals that of the run without a stop, the recording holds every envelope once and in order, and a second restart changes nothing. Another test removes the last five lines of a recording and finds them restored from the journal.
- *The horizon.* The live input keeps three window widths, and the processing of a recording keeps a million by default. A live run is reproduced only if the second processing is told the horizon of three. Without it, a row that the live run dropped as late would be kept; whether the digests then differ was not tested.
- *Not done.* A run on Event Hubs. It needs Azure, and no cluster exists (10.5).

**Definition 10.8 (live resume).** With a store that survives a restart, the live input writes to the journal each envelope together with the position of its source (for Event Hubs the partition and the sequence number, after the envelope) before it processes it. It writes a record that is a duplicate or not an envelope as a position alone, and it writes each moment at which it wrote out its pending rows, so that a restart cuts the steps where the live run cut them. A restarted run reads the last position back, and the source continues after it. An envelope delivered again is dropped by its delivery key; the last 20,000 keys survive a restart. Without a store there is no resume.

**Proposition 10.8 (a killed live run resumes to the state of a run that was never killed).** Let a source deliver envelopes `e_1 … e_N` in a fixed order. Under the premises LR1 to LR4, a run that is killed after it has written `e_k` to its journal and is restarted from its store consumes exactly `e_{k+1} … e_N`, each once, ends in the state digest and the count of input rows of the run that was never killed, and a second restart gives the same state again.
- **LR1 (journal first, whole entries).** The live input writes an envelope to the journal, with the position of its source, before it processes it, and writes every cut of its pending rows to the journal. After a kill, a journal entry is either wholly there or absent. This is premise J1 of Proposition 11.9 for the live path.
- **LR2 (the source resumes).** After a restart the source continues after the journaled position of `e_k`, or delivers again a final part of the envelopes whose delivery keys are among the last 20,000 journaled.
- **LR3.** The premises J2 and J3 of Proposition 11.9.
- **LR4.** The premises B1 to B4 of Theorem 6.2.
- **LR5.** Premise LV2 of Proposition 10.7: an envelope gives the same inputs in every run.

*Proof.* By LR1 the journal at the kill holds `e_1 … e_k` with their positions and the cuts between them, and of `e_{k+1}` nothing (if it holds `e_{k+1}` whole, rename `k`). By Proposition 11.9, whose premises are LR1 and LR3, the restart reproduces the state after the journal: the state of the killed run after `e_k`, with its cuts, the last position in the source and the last 20,000 delivery keys, which are functions of the journal. By LR2 the source then delivers `e_{k+1} … e_N`, possibly after envelopes that are already in the journal; those are dropped by their delivery key (Theorem 10.1). So the resumed run has consumed `e_1 … e_N` in order, each once, cut into steps as the killed run cut them up to `e_k` and in some way after it. By Theorem 6.2(b) and LR4 the state after `e_N` does not depend on the cut, so it is the state of the run that was never killed; the count of rows is a function of the consumed envelopes (LR5). A second restart is Proposition 11.9 again. ∎

The proof has been read, not checked by a program. LR1 is tested with a simulated kill: an exception raised from inside the program, not a kill of the process, and not during the writing of one journal entry. LR2 is a property of the source, tested against a fake source that is a list of envelopes with positions. No test or run reads from the code that reads Event Hubs.

*Tested on examples against a fake source.* The envelopes of the synthetic estate arrive in a shuffled order (up to 12 places). The run is killed after 60 percent of them plus 7, at a moment when the rows of several envelopes are pending, and is restarted from the store, in memory and on a file database, with a state seal every 30 entries and with none. The digest and the count of rows equal those of the run that was never killed; exactly the remaining events are consumed, with no duplicate; and a second restart gives the same digest and the position of the last event. A source that delivers the last 9 events again has them dropped as 9 duplicates. A record that is not an envelope is rejected, counted, and its position kept. **Not tested:** a real Event Hub; more than one partition (the source reads one and warns); a source that delivers more than 20,000 envelopes again; a kill of the process; a kill during the writing of one journal entry.

**The capacity of the forwarder** was measured by the developers of the test environment, one run per row, and not repeated by the authors. Setup: one Event Hubs namespace of the Standard tier with one throughput unit (a purchased unit of capacity, nominally 1 MB/s in), one partition on the stream that the engine reads, and one sender per stream that sends one message at a time.

| Run | Rate | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| flows, sent once | 86 flows/s | 14 min, with one restart of the network observer's relay | clean; memory of the forwarder at most 101 MB, queue near 0 |
| flows, also copied to a second stream | 62 flows/s | 8 min | clean |
| flows, copied | 107 flows/s | 1 h | saturated; 36,000 events dropped, about 9 % sent twice by retries |
| flows, copied | 286 flows/s | 5 min | saturated; 16,000 dropped events reported, memory 244 MB, queue held at 64 MB |

An envelope of a flow is about 4.4 KB. In saturation the delivered rate stays near 125 events/s, about 0.55 MB/s, below the nominal 1 MB/s. The test environment is therefore planned for 125 events/s in total across streams: about 125 flows/s sent once, or 60 flows/s copied. Beyond it the forwarder queues up to 64 MB per stream and then drops the oldest events with a marker `dropped`, whose count is a lower bound while the stream is overloaded.
- *What this means for the targets.* The engine, processing the real recording again, handled about 67,000 records per second in memory (Ch. 13). The transport with one throughput unit and one partition, one send at a time to keep the order, is about 40 times below the target of 5,000 records per second per graph space (Ch. 13). In the test environment the transport, not the engine, limits live ingestion. The network observer itself produces about 325 flows per second (235,858 flows in 725 s), more than the 125 events per second delivered, which is why the forwarder filters at the source, by default to the demo namespaces. The delay from a source to the graph is **not measured** for the live path.
- *Loss is not deletion.* The forwarder reports its own losses and restarts as markers: `start`, `stop`, `relist-start`, `relist-end`, `dropped`, `hubble-reconnect`, `watch-expired`, `watch-error`, `poll-failed`. They are the coverage declarations of Def. 10.4 for the live path, and the engine consumes five of them. A pair `relist-start` to `relist-end` is the complete set of one kind of Kubernetes object (pods, services, machines, deployments, endpoint slices): what the engine knows of that kind and the list did not mention is deleted. A new list that never ends, because of a crash, deletes nothing. `dropped`, `hubble-reconnect` and `poll-failed` mark the window of telemetry that contains them `Partial(forwarderDropped | hubbleReconnect | pollFailed)` with the count dropped; several kinds in one window are joined with `+`. This is Theorem 10.3 for the five kinds of object, provided the forwarder stops its watch of that kind between `relist-start` and `relist-end` (otherwise a newer change could be undone by the list); that is not stated or checked. It is Theorem 10.4 for the windows. The tests use fixtures only: a new list replaces the known set (for four of the five kinds; endpoint slices are not tested); a list without an end and a list of another kind leave a kind alone; the three markers of loss mark their window; two kinds in one window are both named. No recording exercises the markers: the forwarder's records sit in a file that the processing of a recording does not read, and the authors did not run the live input.
- *Markers that have no effect.* `start`, `stop`, `watch-expired` and `watch-error` change nothing in the engine. `watch-expired` is followed by a new list, which repairs the loss. After `dropped` the state is out of date until the next new list; the engine marks the window and does not mark the graph as out of date. A crash without `stop` is not treated as out of date either, except that its unfinished list deletes nothing.

**Definition 10.9 (source progress, heartbeat).** The *progress* of a source is the greatest capture time among the envelopes it has delivered. It is recorded as an input once per source just before each advance of logical time, with a recorded position like any other input. An envelope that carries no row counts as well: a marker of the forwarder, or a *heartbeat*, an envelope that a source sends only to say that it is alive and has nothing to report. The coverage of Def. 7.14 is computed from these records, so it is a recorded fact, and processing a recording again sees the coverage of the live run.
- A source that reports only when something happens (Kubernetes events, the log of management actions) is silent when nothing happens and cannot be told from a source that is down. It needs heartbeats from the forwarder to be covered. The forwarder of the test environment sends none, and the two real recordings hold none, so on them such a source is covered only up to its last record.
- Progress is not a promise that nothing older will come: late rows are still judged by the late horizon (Ch. 7, Def. 7.6). It says how far the source has reported.

## 10.7 What the prototype does differently

- **Streams read.** The diagnostic records of the log of management actions and of the Kubernetes audit log are read from recordings as evidence elements (10.6). The recorded copies of the streams that duplicate the direct sources are skipped. The live input reads one stream of one partition; the other streams are read from recordings only.
- **Markers.** The engine consumes the markers of new lists, `dropped`, `hubble-reconnect` and `poll-failed`; the other four have no effect. Processing a recording is not affected, since the direct sources of the recordings carry no markers. Tested on fixtures only.
- **Horizon of a served recording.** A recording that is served as if it were live uses the horizon of a recording, not the three windows of a live source (Ch. 7, 7.6).
- **Order of a recording.** Records are merged in order of capture time. Each adapter then turns them into rows, and an advance `Advance(λ)` is inserted when the capture clock crosses a step boundary, `λ = floor(capturedAt / step) × step − lateness`. This realizes Theorem 10.6 with the input order by capture time.
- **A flow before its pod.** A flow that names a pod before the pod's row arrives gives the pod an id of its own. When the pod's row arrives, the two identities merge, and the final id does not depend on the order (Proposition 1.8). A conflict is counted only between identities of different entity types; both recordings had none.
- **Event time.** The event time of the network observer does not increase along the stream (the capture starts with history). Late rows revise their windows. The first recording produced no dropped or rejected rows; the newest has one rejected record of the network observer (neither a flow nor a loss message) and no dropped rows.
- **Counts.** On the two real recordings the engine read 245,345 records (none rejected, 5 not consumed) and 160,300 records (1 rejected, 5 not consumed).
