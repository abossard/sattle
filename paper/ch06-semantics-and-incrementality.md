# Ch. 6 Meaning and step-by-step evaluation

**Question.** What does a projection compute, as a mathematical function, and does the engine compute exactly that when it works step by step on a stream of inputs, cut into batches of any size?

**What you can state at the end.** The meaning of a projection is a function of its recorded inputs. Computing it step by step, input after input, gives the same result as computing it from scratch on all inputs so far. Under four stated premises, how the inputs are cut into batches does not change the resulting graph. A replay of the same recorded inputs reproduces the same versions. A recursion with finitely many possible rows ends at its least fixpoint.

This chapter uses identity (Ch. 1), graphs, versions and frontiers (Ch. 3), signed deltas (Ch. 4), and the operators and types of Ch. 5. It refers forward to closed windows of measurements (Ch. 7).

**Running example.** The service graph of Ch. 5 sums the calls from `api` to `db` per minute with a `Rollup` and `sum`. A closed window of one minute holds 230 calls. A record of 6 more calls in that minute arrives late, but early enough that the window may still be revised (the late horizon, Ch. 7), and the window becomes 236. Step by step, the engine adds 230, then subtracts 230 and adds 236. From scratch, it sums all records it has. The chapter shows why both give 236.

## 6.1 Inputs and what they mean

**Definition 6.1 (input log).** The input log of a graph `g` with projection `P` is the sequence `ι = (i₁, i₂, …)` of inputs that `P` consumes, in the order in which the engine recorded them. An input is one of: a batch of rows for a rows port; a graph change (a committed patch `E_r` of an input graph, with its frontier) or a *snapshot* of an input graph, its full state at one version; a logical-time control `λ`; a closed-window record (Def. 7.7) of an input graph or of the projection's own ingestion windows. Each input carries its ingestion sequence number `s`. Changes of the definitions are handled in Ch. 9. Time passing alone changes nothing: only a `λ` input advances logical time.

**Definition 6.2 (Z-sets and their streams).** A *Z-set* is a map from rows to integer weights that is zero except on finitely many rows ([budiu2023]); the Z-sets over rows are `ℤ^(Row)`, the group of Def. 4.6 over rows. For each port, rows travel as Z-sets. A stream is a sequence of such maps indexed by input position. The *integral* `I` sums a stream prefix, the *difference* `D` takes successive differences, so `D ∘ I = I ∘ D = id` on streams that start at `0`. The state of a graph input port after `n` inputs is the integral of the facts of the patches received. It is a materializable Z-set, the embedding `χ(G)` of a graph (Def. 4.7).

*Example:* the window "230 calls from `api` to `db`" enters as the row with weight `+1`. Its revision enters as the same row with weight `−1` and the revised row, 236 calls, with weight `+1`. The integral after both holds only the revised row.

**Definition 6.3 (denotation of an operator).** Each operator kind has a function from the Z-sets of its in-ports to the Z-set of its out-port (the *batch semantics*):
- `Match`: the Z-set of variable bindings of the pattern over the facts of the graph port. A node pattern is a *selection* on entity facts, the facts that satisfy a condition; an edge pattern a join of a relationship fact with its endpoint entity facts (an endpoint that is absent from `G` does not bind, so dangling relationships bind only patterns that do not require the endpoint entity).
- `Filter`, `Map`: pointwise on rows, `Map` by evaluating expressions of the closed function set. `Union`: the sum of its inputs (Def. 5.10).
- `Join`: the *equijoin*, the pairs of rows whose join keys are equal, with weights multiplied. `Distinct`: weight `1` where the weight is positive.
- `Group`, `Rollup`: per key, aggregates over the rows with weights as multiplicities. `Traverse`: bounded reachability over relationship facts by **shortest distance**. For a start `s` the output has one row per element `t` whose distance `d(s, t)`, the length of a shortest path in the given direction, satisfies `minDepth ≤ d(s, t) ≤ maxDepth`, and the depth column is that distance. `d(s, s) = 0`, so a start is in its own result only with `minDepth = 0`, also when it lies on a cycle. It is not the union over path lengths: that reading gives several depths per pair, counts walks on cycles, and differs from this one for `minDepth ≥ 2` and on a cycle through the start.
- `Window`: assigns `windowStart` and `windowEnd` by the aligned grid (Def. 7.5). A window is *closed* by a `λ` input (Def. 7.6) and releases its rows then.
- `Fixpoint`: the least fixpoint of the body (Def. 6.9).
- `Series`, `Rollup` on windows, `Delta`, `Threshold`: Def. 6.7.
- Sinks: `EmitEntity`, `EmitRelationship`, `Link` produce *fragments* `(key, partial state)` with weights.

**Definition 6.4 (denotation of a projection, output graph).** `⟦P⟧` maps the integrated input state `S` (a family of Z-sets, one per port) to the output graph `O(S)` by composing the operator functions along the operator graph and then *materializing* the fragments. For each element key `k`, the support `σ(k) = Σ weights of fragments for k`. The element is present iff `σ(k) > 0` and some create fragment exists for it (update fragments for absent keys have no effect). Weights add in any order: a Z-set has no order (Def. 6.2), so a retraction may reach a sink before the insertion it cancels, inside one batch or across batches, and `σ(k)` may be negative in between. A sink is linear in the weights and keeps a negative support; it does not drop it. Its state merges the property assignments of its positive fragments, and the merge is a function of the multiset of fragments: no order of sinks, statements or arrival enters it, and an update fragment does not override a create fragment.
- *Property conflict.* If the positive fragments of a key assign more than one value to a property, the property is **absent** on the element, and the key, the property and the values are a *conflict*: reported with the commit and counted.
- *Type conflict.* If the positive create fragments of one key disagree on the type of the element, or on the endpoints of a relationship, the element is absent and the conflict is reported: an element has one type and one pair of endpoints, and no order of sinks picks one.
- *Uniqueness conflict.* If more than one relationship with positive support has the same `(source, target, type)`, the one with the smallest `rid` (Def. 5.9) is present, the others are absent and reported as conflicts, so the result satisfies `U`.
- *No failure of the change.* A conflict does not fail the change: one bad key must not stop a whole graph.

- *What batch invariance requires* (Theorem 6.2). Only that the outcome does not depend on arrival order or on how the inputs are cut into batches, which any function of the fragment multiset gives. "The last arrival wins" violates it. "The last sink wins" would satisfy it and is rejected for another reason: the meaning of a module would depend on the order of its statements, which a refactoring does not preserve (Proposition 5.7).

*Example:* two rules of the shop give the service `db` the owner `t1` and `t2`. The service is present without `owner`, and the conflict is reported with the version. A third rule that also says `t1` changes nothing: the values still disagree.

**Definition 6.5 (incremental form).** For a function `Q` on Z-set streams, `Q^Δ = D ∘ Q ∘ I`: integrate the changes, apply `Q`, and take the change of the result. For a projection, one **step** of the engine reads input `i_{n+1}` and produces the change `Q^Δ` of `⟦P⟧` for that input, which becomes the edit set of the next commit (Def. 3.9), possibly empty. The point of the incremental form is cost: a good strategy computes `Q^Δ` from the change alone and from a small kept state, without recomputing `Q` on everything.

## 6.2 Frontiers, cuts and measurements

**Definition 6.6 (frontier order, consistent cut).** Frontiers are ordered as in Def. 3.10 (entry by entry, over the input streams of the sources and the input graphs). A *cut* of a graph space assigns each graph `g` a revision `r_g`. A cut is **causally consistent** iff for every derived graph `g` and input graph `g'`, `φ_{g, r_g}.gin[g'] ≤ r_{g'}` (no graph reflects an input revision that the cut does not contain). A cut is **aligned** iff in addition all graphs reflect the same master revision. The frontier records what a commit depended on; it is not a cut. Aligned cuts are not built (Ch. 11). How to record one consistent state of several computers that run at the same time is the subject of [chandy1985]. The word *frontier* comes from timely dataflow ([murray2013]), a model of computation over streams, where it has a partial-order meaning that the engine's frontier shares for the input streams of the sources.

**Definition 6.7 (telemetry operators as folds).** For one key and a sequence of closed-window records ordered by `windowStart`:
- `Delta`: `inc_j = x_j − x_{j-1}` if `x_j ≥ x_{j-1}`, else `x_j` (reset), `inc₁ = 0`.
- `Rollup` (additive functions): the aggregate of Def. 7.8 over the records grouped by key and window.
- `Series`: the closed windows in `[frontier − within, frontier)`. The look-back decides which windows a later operator reads as data and which keys have a row. It does not bound the history of a state.
- `Threshold`: the state machine of Def. 7.10 over the current *episode* of the key, started in `initial`; one row per key that has a window in the look-back, whose `status` changes on a state change. The state is not a function of the windows in the look-back alone: a key that entered `high` stays `high` while its values stay inside the band, also after the windows in which it entered have left the look-back.
Their batch semantics is the function of the *set* of closed windows per key, in `windowStart` order, each with its current value while it is in the retained range and with the value it had when it left that range after that, together with the frontier. A revised window (late policy `revise`) is a retraction plus an insertion, and the incremental form recomputes the affected key from the revised window onward, over the retained windows and from the carried state of Def. 7.10.

**Definition 6.8 (telemetry progress in the frontier).** The entry `gin[g']` of a frontier is a pair `(r, tw)`: the consumed revision `r` of the structural plane of the input graph `g'` and the telemetry watermark `tw`, the ingestion sequence of the last closed-window record of `g'` consumed. `Series` over graph port `p` at an input reads exactly the closed-window records of `g'` with sequence up to `tw`. This is the telemetry frontier of Def. 7.11 for cross-graph reads. Without this pin, a read of another graph's windows would not be determined by the frontier.

**Definition 6.9 (least fixpoint).** For a `Fixpoint` with body `f` over rows of type `R_rel`, `lfp(f, seed) = ⋃_{n ≥ 0} f^n(seed)` where `f^{n+1}(X) = X ∪ f(f^n(X))`, as sets (weights collapsed by `Distinct`).

## 6.3 Theorems

**Theorem 6.1 (step by step equals from scratch).** Let `Q` be the composition of the operator functions of Def. 6.3 of a projection. For the operators of the kernel, the incremental strategies of 6.4 compute `Q^Δ = D ∘ Q ∘ I`. In particular after every input `n`, the integral of the output deltas equals `⟦P⟧` applied to the integrated inputs, that is the batch result. For some operators the batch semantics has one more argument: `Retain`, `Series` and the release of a closed `Window` take the frontier, `Threshold` takes the recorded sequence of its closed windows (Def. 7.10), and a `Series` with a fill or a time-to-live takes the recorded progress of its sources and, for each element, the advance since which the element is present (Def. 7.14 to 7.16). The theorem holds with those arguments; for `Threshold` and for such a `Series` the batch result is therefore not a function of the integrated input alone.
The proof is by induction on the network of operators, relative to two theorems of [budiu2023] that are stated in it and not proved again (their statements were checked against the published text). Two things are outside it. (1) An unbounded `Fixpoint` needs the nested construction of [budiu2023], Section 5, and [mcsherry2013]; the prototype has only the bounded unrolling, which the proof covers. (2) That the program's strategy for each operator (6.4) is the rule proved here is a property of the program. It is tested by comparing the prototype with an independent implementation that computes every result from scratch, on generated inputs, under every cut into batches and with reordering inside a batch (Ch. 14, 14.6).

*Proof.* Streams are sequences of Z-sets indexed by the input position. `I` is the running sum and `D` its inverse (`D(s)[n] = s[n] − s[n−1]`), so `I ∘ D = D ∘ I = id`. `z⁻¹` is the delay by one position.
- *Chain rule.* `(Q₂ ∘ Q₁)^Δ = D Q₂ Q₁ I = D Q₂ (I D) Q₁ I = (D Q₂ I)(D Q₁ I) = Q₂^Δ ∘ Q₁^Δ`. The same computation gives `(Q₁ + Q₂)^Δ = Q₁^Δ + Q₂^Δ` for a fan-in by addition. The *plan* of a projection is the network of operators that is evaluated, after each `lift` is replaced (Ch. 5, 5.8). A plan is an acyclic composition of operators, so it is enough to show `o^Δ = D ∘ o ∘ I` for the strategy of every operator kind `o`.
- *Linear operators.* Cited: for an operator `Q` that is linear (`Q(a + b) = Q(a) + Q(b)`) and applied position by position, `Q^Δ = Q` ([budiu2023], Theorem 3.3). `Filter`, `Map`, `Union`, window assignment and the emits are of this kind. Their strategy applies the operator to the delta, which is `Q^Δ`. `Values` is a constant and not linear: its output stream is its rows at the first position and nothing after, which is `D` of the constant stream.
- *`Match`.* A node pattern is a selection on entity facts, which is linear. An edge pattern is a join of relationship facts with the facts of their endpoint entities, which is bilinear, followed by a map to the binding row (Def. 6.3). The chain rule and the two cited theorems apply.
- *Bilinear operators.* Cited: for an operator `×` that is linear in each argument and applied position by position, `(a × b)^Δ = a × b + z⁻¹(I(a)) × b + a × z⁻¹(I(b))`, where `a` and `b` are the delta streams ([budiu2023], Theorem 3.4). `Join` is bilinear. Its strategy keeps `I(a)` and `I(b)` as indexes and evaluates the three terms. `Traverse` with bound `maxDepth` is a composition of `maxDepth` joins and a union, followed by the minimum of the depth per pair of start and reached element and the test against `minDepth`; the joins are covered by the chain rule and the minimum is a per-key function (below).
- *Per-key functions of the integrated state.* `Distinct`, `Group` with any aggregate, and the fold `Rollup` (Def. 6.7) are not linear as operators on rows: a changed aggregate retracts one output row and inserts another. Each is a function `h` applied key by key to the integrated input of that key: `o(I(a))[n](κ) = h(I(a)[n] restricted to κ)`. The strategy keeps the integrated input per key (a multiset with weights or the fold state; for `count` and `sum` the running value, which determines the aggregate because these are additive), which is `I(a)` restricted to the key because integration is linear, re-evaluates `h` for the keys that the delta touches, and emits the difference between the new and the old value of `h` for those keys. For an untouched key the integrated input is unchanged, so `h` is unchanged and the difference is zero. The emitted stream is therefore `D(o(I(a)))`, which is `o^Δ` by definition.
- *Operators filtered by the frontier.* `Retain`, `Series` and the release of closed windows have a batch semantics of the form `o(S, λ) = S` restricted to `live(λ)`, where `live(λ)` is a set of rows that depends on the frontier: the live rows (Def. 5.15), the windows in `[λ − within, λ)`, the rows of the windows that `λ` has closed. The strategy keeps the integrated input, passes a row delta iff its row is in `live(λ)` at its position, and on an advance from `λ` to `λ'` emits `+S(r)` for the rows that enter `live` and `−S(r)` for the rows that leave it. By induction over the positions, `I(out)[n]` is `I(in)[n]` restricted to `live(λ_n)`, which is `o` on the integrated input: the induction is written out for `Retain` in Proposition 5.9, where rows only leave, and it does not use this theorem. Which rows count as input of a window is fixed by the late rule (premise B2 of Theorem 6.2).
- *`Series` with a fill or a time-to-live.* Its output at a position is the stored windows of the look-back, the filled records and the no-data records. By Def. 7.14 to 7.16 these are a function of five things: the stored windows, the structural input, the frontier, the recorded progress of the feeding sources, and `since(k)` for every element with a row. The first three are the previous case. Progress is an input like any other, and only its greatest value per source counts, so it is a function of the integrated input. `since(k)` is set and dropped at advances only: it is a function of the recorded sequence of advances together with the presence of `k` at each of them, and not of the integrated input. The strategy keeps one instant per present element, updates it at an advance exactly as Def. 7.14 says, computes the output from the five things, and emits its change, which is `D` of the batch result. Nothing is kept per filled window. That the same value comes out for every cut of the input is premise B4 of Theorem 6.2.
- *`Threshold`.* The input of the operator is the windows of a key that are in the look-back, and a window that grows old is retracted from that input, while the state must not forget it (Def. 6.7). The state is therefore not a function of the integrated input of the operator, and the per-key case does not apply. The batch semantics is the fold `F` of Def. 7.10 over the recorded sequence of closed windows of the key: the windows of the current episode in `windowStart` order, each with its current value while it is in the retained range, and with the value it had on leaving the range after that. `F` is a fold from the left, so `F(w₁ … w_n) = fold(c_j, w_{j+1} … w_n)` with the carried state `c_j = F(w₁ … w_j)`. The strategy keeps `c_j` for the newest window behind the range and the windows after it. A value behind the range never changes, so `c_j` is never recomputed, and a revision inside the range folds again from `c_j`. The strategy thus holds `F` after every input and emits the change of the key's row, which is `D` of the batch result. A recomputation from the integrated input under the final structure would differ whenever the structure changed after a window left the range; that is not the batch semantics. `Delta` has the same shape and is not built.
- *Materialization.* The sinks are linear and produce fragments. The output graph is the function of Def. 6.4 of the integrated fragments, per element key (presence, type, merged properties) and per uniqueness tuple; the per-key case applies with those keys.
- *`Fixpoint`, as built.* The engine evaluates a `Fixpoint` with bound `max` as the `max`-fold unrolling of its body: a composition of `max` copies of the body operators with a union and a `Distinct`. It is a plan without a cycle, so the chain rule and the cases above apply.
- *`Lift`* has no strategy: it is replaced by other operators before evaluation (Ch. 5, 5.8). *`Link`* emits no row; its effect on identity is the subject of Proposition 1.8.
By induction on the plan, the incremental evaluation computes `Q^Δ`, and integrating both sides gives `I(Q^Δ(a)) = Q(I(a))`: the integral of the output deltas is the batch result on the integrated input. ∎

**Theorem 6.2 (batch invariance).** (a) The output log of a projection does not depend on how the input sequence is cut into pieces for delivery (its *chunking*), as long as the engine evaluates the same inputs as the same steps. (b) If the engine evaluates `g` consecutive inputs as one step, the output state after the step equals the state after the last of those inputs processed one at a time, and the committed patch of the step has the edit set of the net composition of the per-input patches, `net(p_{b+1} then … then p_{b+g})`; if that edit set is empty the step commits nothing. Revision boundaries between the batched inputs are lost, as a squash (Def. 4.9): the revision numbers after the step differ from those of the one-at-a-time run, and so does whatever is recorded per commit (the conflicts reported with a commit, evidence histories). An engine that makes every piece of the delivery one step, as the prototype's replay does with its batch size, is in case (b): its states agree across chunk sizes and its logs do not.
It is proved below from Theorem 6.1 and four premises. The first three are not technicalities: each was found because a replay of a real recording gave different results for different batch sizes, and each was then fixed in the prototype (6.6).

**Premises.**
- **B1 (one writer per telemetry key).** For every key (element, property, window start) of a telemetry sink, at most one row reaches the sink per window close. Group equality must therefore be the same equality as the key of the emitted element, after canonicalization. A store that keeps the last value written makes the value depend on emission order, and emission order depends on chunking. Several rows that write one value for one key are one value with counted support, and the window stays while a row supports it. The premise is about rows written under one id: rows that reach one window from groups formed under two ids of one merged class are two contributions and are combined by the rule of Law 7.5, item 3. The intended behavior when the premise is broken is that of Def. 6.4: two different values for one key are a conflict, the window has no value and the conflict is reported; storing the greatest or the last value hides it.
- **B2 (late rows are a function of logical time).** A row for a window whose state has been dropped (beyond the late horizon, Ch. 7, Def. 7.6) is dropped and counted. It neither revises nor recreates the window. Whether a row is late depends only on its event time and the logical frontier at its recorded position, never on the batch it travelled in. A row that an operator derives while a logical-time advance is processed is judged against the frontier before that advance: the advance that closes a window does not make the rows derived from the closing late.
- **B3 (identity binding is independent of arrival order).** After any prefix of the recorded input, the identity of every element, as seen through `Rep`, is the same whatever the chunking. This holds when the representative of an entity is the smallest id minted for any key that denotes it, whether or not that key was used before (Proposition 1.8). Two weaker rules do not give it. With "first binding wins" and a counted conflict it holds only when no two keys of one entity reach the catalog in an order that depends on chunking. With a merge of only the ids already in use, the final id still depends on whether a key that nothing had used yet arrives before or after the key that resolved the entity. The prototype had each of these rules in turn, and each was found by a digest that differed (6.6).

- **B4 (a step is its inputs in order, where the semantics is a function of the sequence).** `Threshold` fixes the value of a window when it leaves the retained range, under the structure of that moment (Def. 7.10), so its state is a function of the recorded sequence and not of the integrated input (Theorem 6.1). Within one step, logical-time advances and the changes of structure around them must take effect on carried state in their recorded order. The same holds for a link and the telemetry rows around it, where the merged window is not a monoid value (Law 7.5, item 3, case (ii)), and for a `Series` with a fill or a time-to-live, whose `since(k)` is set and dropped at advances (Def. 7.14). The first three premises make the rest of the state a function of the integrated input; this one covers the part that is a function of the sequence.
- *A case that is a function of the sequence.* A telemetry window that is not a monoid value under its combine rule (a `mean` stored as one number, a `last` without an order) on an element that merges: whether it has a value depends on whether the rows were processed before or after the link (Law 7.5, item 3). It is the same under every chunking by B4, and it is never a wrong number: the window has the batch value or none.
- *Outside the statement.* Evidence histories (Def. 9.2) are defined from commits and so depend on the steps. The theorem is about graph state and telemetry.

*Proof.* (a) The engine consumes records in the recorded order and assigns the ingestion sequence per record. Batches carry records and are not units of success or failure: a malformed record is rejected without dropping the rest. So the sequence `ι` and the per-record results do not depend on chunking. (b) By Theorem 6.1, the output state after any prefix of inputs is `⟦P⟧(I(inputs))`, a function of the integrated state, hence the same for the batch and for the sequence. Consecutive committed patches compose (Theorem 3.1, Def. 4.2), and `net(p then q then …)` is `toPatch` of the signed delta between the two states (Theorem 4.8). The batch patch is this canonical net transition. Stateful operators consume closed windows in `windowStart` order within a step, so their fold is unchanged. A record rejected inside a batch is rejected individually, so the batch contains the same accepted records. Theorem 6.1 gives a function of the integrated state only for output that is a function of it. B1 makes the telemetry value of a key a function of the rows of its group, not of write order. B2 makes the set of rows that count for a window a function of logical time. B3 makes the identities of the emitted elements a function of the recorded sequence. B4 makes the carried state of a `Threshold` after a step the state after its inputs taken one at a time, which is the function of the sequence that Theorem 6.1 gives. Without them the output is a function of the integrated state and of the batch boundaries, and (b) fails. ∎

*How the premises are checked.*
- **B1** is tested: one resource written under two spellings of its id, in one batch, in two batches, and in two batches in the other order, gives one window with the right value. With the fix taken out (the group keyed by the raw id), all three cases fail.
- **B2** is tested: four cases of rows beyond the late horizon are dropped and counted and replace nothing, and two rows inside the horizon revise their window. With the drop taken out, the four cases fail and the two still pass.
- **B3** is tested on examples and on the two real recordings, not in general. Tests check the laws of merging and the survivor under all orders of three merges and of three keys. Another test has one synthetic input in two orders of arrival (the pod row before the flows that name the pod, or after them) at batch sizes 1, 2, 5 and 2,000, with equal digests, and checks that the number of merges differs between the two orders, so the test is not empty. The replays of both recordings at five batch sizes give one digest each, with the same number of merges at every size (35 and 43; Ch. 14, 14.5). No generator of random orders of arrival exists yet.
- **B4** is exercised by the comparison with the independent implementation, which cuts the input of every scenario at every position and compares with a result folded over the sequence. Whether a scenario puts a change of structure and an advance into one step is not yet confirmed.
- B1 to B3 together are exercised by every replay of a recording at several batch sizes, and by a test that delivers inputs in shuffled order with random points at which pending rows are written.

**Theorem 6.3 (replay determinism).** The output log of a graph is a function of the recorded input sequence of its projection and the pinned definitions. Two runs from the same state with the same recorded sequence, the same definitions and the same logical-time controls produce equal commit records. The recorded sequence is the sequence of inputs of Def. 6.1, in which a batch of rows is one input: two runs that cut the same records into different batches have different input sequences, and Theorem 6.2(b), not this theorem, relates them. Both runs use one engine release, because the denotations of Def. 6.3 are compiled (Proposition 5.4).
It rests on F1 (Theorem 3.5), Proposition 5.4 and Theorem 6.1.

*Proof.* Induction over the input sequence. The transition from state and input to the next state and commit is a composition of functions: (1) operator denotations are functions of Z-sets, which are unordered, and the serialization of the resulting edit set has a canonical order (Theorem 4.4); (2) expressions use the closed function set (Proposition 5.4), so they depend on row values only; (3) identity allocation is a pure function (Theorem 1.4) and resolution depends on the binding set, itself a function of earlier inputs (Theorem 1.5); (4) logical time enters only through `λ` inputs; (5) a conflict is resolved by a function of the fragment multiset (Def. 6.4) and a runtime fault is local to its row (Def. 5.19), so neither has an arrival-based tie-break and neither fails the event; (6) a `Fixpoint` is a bounded unrolling, a function of its input (Theorem 6.1), and what its bound cuts off is a function of the data; (7) telemetry enters only as closed-window records with sequence numbers and a pinned watermark (Def. 6.8, Theorem 7.3). Different projections may advance concurrently, but each consumes its own recorded sequence, which includes the graph-change inputs of upstream graphs at their recorded positions. The engine does not promise identical outputs for different interleavings of independent sources: the interleaving is part of the recorded input. ∎

**Theorem 6.4 (a snapshot and the following records rebuild every version).** A reader that takes a snapshot of graph `g` at `r_s`, one that the store has marked complete (*sealed*), and then applies the commit records `r_s + 1, r_s + 2, …` it receives, holds `G_r` after each, for every `r`. A reader of a scoped graph (Def. 3.12) holds the scoped state in the same way. A snapshot followed by a squashed batch reconstructs only the final boundary.
It is proved relative to the properties of a store stated in Ch. 11.

*Proof.* `G_r = apply(E_r, …apply(E_{r_s+1}, Snap(r_s)))` (Theorem 11.1(a)). The store delivers the records after the snapshot exactly as `r_s + 1, r_s + 2, …`, in order, once each, or an explicit `Gap` (Theorem 11.5), so no record is lost between the snapshot and the first record. A `Gap` requires a new snapshot and is never a successful partial reconstruction. A squashed batch is `net` of the composed patches (Def. 4.4), which maps the state at its `from` to its `to` and says nothing about intermediate states (Theorem 4.8). ∎

**Theorem 6.5 (recursion ends at the least fixpoint).** Let a `Fixpoint` body satisfy the admitted fragment (operators `Match`, `Filter`, `Map`, `Join`, `Union(requireEqual)`, `Distinct`, and `Traverse` with `minDepth ≤ 1` whose depth column is not read; no `IdOf`) and let the active domain be finite: every field of `R_rel` ranges over a finite set `A` (entities of the port `over`, values of the seed, literals of the program). Then `f` is monotone, the iteration reaches `lfp(f, seed)` in at most `|A|^k + 1` steps for rows of `k` fields, and `lfp` is the unique least fixpoint.
It holds under the finite-domain condition F5 (the remark below). With `maxIterations ≥ |A|^k + 1` and `maxFacts ≥ |A|^k` evaluation never reaches a limit.

*Limits.* What the prototype guarantees is less: its `Fixpoint` is an unrolling with a bound `max` between 1 and 64, so it always ends, and it returns `lfp(f, seed)` only when the least fixpoint is reached within `max` steps. F5 is not checked, and the tests use acyclic inputs only.

*Example:* the services that `web` depends on, directly or not: seed `{(web, api)}`, body "if `(x, y)` and `y DependsOn z` then `(x, z)`". The first step adds `(web, db)`, the second adds nothing, and the fixpoint is reached. Here `A` is the three services and `k = 2`.

*Proof.* Each admitted operator is monotone in set semantics: `Match`, `Join` and `Map` are unions of images of their input rows, `Traverse` without its depth column and with `minDepth ≤ 1` is the set of pairs at distance at most `maxDepth` (without the start itself for `minDepth = 1`), which only grows when edges are added (with a larger `minDepth`, or with the depth in the row, a new edge can shorten a distance and remove a row, so the operator is not monotone), `Filter` has a predicate that depends on a row only, `Distinct` and `Union(requireEqual)` act on sets. So `f` is monotone and extensive after `X ∪ f(X)`. The sets `f^n(seed)` form an ascending chain inside the finite lattice `2^{A^k}`, which stabilizes after at most `|A|^k` strict increases. The stable value is a fixpoint, and any fixpoint containing the seed contains every `f^n(seed)` by induction, so it is the least. ∎

*Remark (the finite-domain condition F5).* The typing rule of `Fixpoint` requires entity fields to come from the finite port `over` but allows `Map` in the body, so an arithmetic field (a depth counter, `d + 1`) is not finite, and then only `maxFacts` and `maxIterations` bound the evaluation. F5 is a typing condition proposed here: assignments in a fixpoint body copy fields or use literals, or the program declares a bound domain. Without F5, Theorem 6.5 does not hold, and the bound only cuts the result short (Def. 5.19).

**Theorem 6.6 (retraction under recursion).** (a) Re-evaluating an affected `Fixpoint`, one whose input changed, from the integrated inputs after any retraction is correct. (b) Support counting is not correct on cyclic derivations.
(a) follows from Theorem 6.1, (b) is shown by a counterexample. Neither is tested on a cyclic input, and the cost of retraction under recursion is not measured.

*Proof.* (a) By Theorem 6.1 the correct incremental output is `D ∘ ⟦P⟧ ∘ I`, and evaluation of `⟦P⟧` on the integrated state is the definition. (b) Take `r(a) ← base(a)`, `r(b) ← r(a)`, `r(a) ← r(b)`. With `base(a)` present, `r(a)` has support 2 (base and `r(b)`) and `r(b)` has support 1. Retracting `base(a)` leaves `r(a)` with support 1 and `r(b)` with support 1, both positive, although neither is derivable. The correct result is empty. ∎

*Remark (literature, not used).* Efficient maintenance of recursive views under retraction exists: DRed (delete and rederive) and counting for non-recursive views ([gupta1993]), and nested incremental evaluation with partially ordered versions ([mcsherry2013], [budiu2023] Section 5). The engine does not use them: its bounded unrolling is maintained operator by operator (Theorem 6.1).

**Proposition 6.7 (cross-graph telemetry reads are deterministic).** A `Series` over a graph port reads a set of closed windows that is a function of the frontier entry `gin[g'] = (r, tw)`.
*Proof.* By Def. 6.8 the `Series` reads exactly the closed windows of `g'` up to the watermark `tw` of the frontier entry, and the revision `r` fixes the elements they belong to. ∎ Theorem 7.3 uses this step.

**Proposition 6.8 (stateful telemetry operators under revision).** For `Delta` and `Threshold`, the output after a revision of window `j` of key `k` inside the revisable range is the fold over the revised sequence, so the emitted change is the difference of the key's outputs before and after. A correction behind that range is dropped and counted (premise B2) and changes nothing.
*Proof.* By Def. 6.7 the output for a key is the fold over its sequence of closed windows, so after a revision it is the fold over the revised sequence, and the change emitted is the difference of the two outputs. Behind the range, B2 applies. ∎

A correction can flip `status` at a window earlier than the one just closed, so a revised window is not silent. The late policies `reject` and `ignore` avoid corrections.

*Example:* the threshold of Ch. 5 had entered `degraded` after three windows at or above 5 percent. A late correction lowers the second of them to 3 percent. The run of three is broken, no later run of three exists, the state was never `degraded`, and the edge's `status` goes back to `ok`.

## 6.4 Incremental strategy per operator

For each operator the engine keeps a small state and computes the change of its output from the change of its input. The classes follow [budiu2023]; their costs are in Ch. 11 (Fact 11.1) and Ch. 13 (Theorem 13.1).

| Operator | Strategy | State |
|---|---|---|
| `Filter`, `Map`, `Union`, emits, `Window` assignment | linear: apply to the delta batch | none |
| `Match` edges, `Join` | bilinear: new left against old right, then new right against the new left | an index of the rows seen so far, by entity or by join key |
| `Traverse` | per start element a search outward, one step of depth at a time, to the depth bound, recomputed only when a visited element or edge changed, only the difference emitted | visited set per start |
| `Group`, `Rollup` with `count`, `sum` | per-key accumulators updated by signed deltas | one per key (and window) |
| `Group`, `Rollup` with `min`, `max`, `mean`, `weightedMean`, `any`, `all`, `last` | per-key ordered multiset, or recompute the affected key | per key |
| `Distinct` | weight per row, emit on `0 ↔ positive` transitions | weight map |
| `Delta` | per-key fold over ordered closed windows | previous value per key |
| `Threshold` | per-key state machine over ordered closed windows | the windows of the retained range and one carried state per key |
| `Fixpoint` (design, not built) | *semi-naive* iteration, in which each round joins only the rows that are new since the previous round; re-derivation of affected keys on retraction | relation plus support |
| `Fixpoint` (engine) | bounded unrolling of `distinct(R union step(R))` into `max` copies of the step, so every operator in it stays incremental; facts that need more than `max` steps are not derived, and `maxFacts` is recorded and not enforced | one `Distinct` and one step per level |
| `Series` | reads new closed windows in the order they close, retracts windows leaving `within` | the position reached per key; with fill or time-to-live also one instant per present element and the progress per source |

Evaluation never reads the catalog graph. When a projection is made active, ids are replaced by small integers, the subtypes of each type are precomputed, rows are stored one column at a time, expressions are translated for a program that evaluates them on many rows at once, chains of linear operators are merged into one, and the strategy above is chosen per operator. Because each projection reads its inputs in order, work can run at the same time only in stages inside a batch, each stage working on what the stage before it has finished, and across independent projections (Theorem 13.3). Splitting one projection by entity is not built.

## 6.5 Example

Reachability as the admitted recursion: `R` is the seed together with every pair `(x, z)` such that `(x, y)` is in `R` and `E` holds an edge from `y` to `z`. That is, `R` is joined with `E` on the middle element and the middle element is then dropped. Here `E` is the relationship facts of type `DependsOn` and the active domain is the entities of the port `over`. Inserting `E(c, d)` into a state with `R ⊇ {(a, c)}` derives `(a, d)` by semi-naive iteration in one round. Retracting an edge on a cycle `a → b → a` that was the only support of both `r(a)` and `r(b)` is the case of Theorem 6.6 (b): counting supports keeps both, recomputing the affected keys removes both.

## 6.6 What the prototype does differently, and how the premises were found

**How B1 and B2 were found.** On the 95-minute real recording, a replay in batches of 50 records gave a different state digest than the other batch sizes: one window of the counts of management actions had the value 2 and not 3, and all graph states were equal. There were two causes. (1) The log of management actions writes one resource under two spellings of its id, which differ in the case of one word. A `Group` keyed by the raw text made two aggregates for one element and window, the store of measurements kept the last one written, and so the value depended on the order of writing. That breaks B1; the definitions now group by the lower-case id. (2) A row for a window whose state had already been dropped, beyond the late horizon, created a new window that replaced the value written earlier. That breaks B2; such a row is now dropped and counted. Since the fix the digest is equal at every batch size.

**How B3 was found.** After that fix, batches of one record still gave a different digest on the same recording: eight `Calls` relationships of the master graph differed in the id of one end, and the counters of identity differed. The cause was the rule "the first binding wins": which of two keys of one entity was bound first depended on the batching. A first fix merged two identities that denote one entity. The digest was then equal at five batch sizes, but the number of merges still depended on the size, a sign that the final ids were not yet independent of the order. The second fix made the representative the smallest id computed for any key of the entity (Ch. 1, Proposition 1.8). Since then each recording has one digest and one number of merges (35 and 43) at all five batch sizes (Ch. 14, 14.5).

**Where the prototype differs from this chapter.**
- **Closed windows are recomputed, not recorded.** Windows close inside the projection, and a replay recomputes closed windows from the raw rows. Closed-window records are not inputs of their own (Ch. 7, Def. 7.7). Replay is deterministic because the raw rows are the recorded input.
- **The late horizon.** On a live source a closed window stays revisable for 3 window widths; a recorded input, replayed or served, keeps a million (Ch. 7).
- **`Fixpoint`** is a bounded unrolling with `max` levels, not the iteration of Def. 6.9. Theorem 6.5 applies when `max` is at least the longest derivation; a smaller `max` gives part of the closure (with `max` 1 on a chain of four nodes, 5 of the 6 pairs). Unrolling has no cycle, which is why the counterexample of Theorem 6.6 (b) does not arise. No test retracts on a cyclic input. The health model of Ch. 7 uses `Traverse` and not `Fixpoint`.
- **`Delta`** is not built. Merged operators are run by the program that evaluates many rows at once; they are not translated into machine code.
- **Conflicts** are resolved as Def. 6.4 says, counted per run, and sent right after the commit in which they appear, change or end.
- **A `Group` is not re-keyed when two identities merge.** Theorem 6.1 gives the result in which every identity is resolved after the input, so the rows of two keys of one entity are one group once the keys are linked. The prototype keys a `Group` by the values on its rows and resolves identity where it writes. After a merge two groups stay two, and rows that arrive later with the surviving id open a third. Where they write, equal values are one value and different values are a property conflict (Def. 6.4), so a count or a sum per entity is absent where the result of Theorem 6.1 is the count or sum over both keys. This is a function of the recorded sequence and the same under every batching (B4), but it is not the result of Theorem 6.1. It does not arise in a graph that reads another graph through `Match`, because there a merge arrives as a change of the input graph. The comparison with the independent implementation has no scenario with a `Group` keyed by an identity that merges, so it does not see the difference.
