# Ch. 12 What a person sees: the viewer and documents

**Question.** How does a picture or a report of a graph stay exactly what the engine holds, version by version, and how can a person edit definitions through a picture rather than through text?

**What you can state at the end.** A graph has no outputs of its own. Everything a person sees is made by a *subscriber*: a program that takes the graph at one version and then applies each later patch in order. A subscriber that applies a patch only on the version it expects holds the engine's state at some version and never skips or repeats one. A document is a derived graph whose elements are blocks of text, rendered by one fixed function, so equal graph states give equal documents and equal digests. Definitions are graphs too, so the same mechanism shows and edits them. The viewer that the project built uses all of this; what it was tested on is stated with each claim.

The numbers of the definitions and statements of this chapter are kept from an earlier version, so they are not all in reading order.

This chapter uses identity (Ch. 1), graphs, versions and snapshots (Ch. 3), patches (Ch. 4), Theorem 6.4, series and the view of an element (Ch. 7), provenance (Ch. 9), and following a log (Ch. 11).

**Running example.** A person opens the shop's workload graph (Ch. 7, 7.4) in the viewer. The database server goes down; the server, the database service, `api`, `web`, the gateway and the workload turn `Unhealthy`, and the colors of their nodes change without a reload. The same person reads a one-page report of the shop's deployments, at the current version and at an earlier one.

## 12.1 Subscribers of a graph

**Definition 12.1 (feed of a graph).** The *feed* of graph `g` is a snapshot `Snap(r_s)` with its seal, followed by the commit records `r_s + 1, r_s + 2, …` from `Follow` (Def. 11.3), and, separately, the feed of its measurements (closed windows and reads of series, Def. 11.4). A feed has no gaps (Theorem 11.5), or it says `Gap`.

**Definition 12.2 (replica).** A *replica* of graph `g` is a copy `R` held by a subscriber. It starts from a snapshot at `r_s` and applies each patch it receives with the guard of Def. 3.7. A patch whose version is not the successor of the replica's, or whose before-states do not match, is **rejected and reported**: it is never applied silently, and never dropped without being counted.

**Definition 12.3 (the seam of the viewer).** The viewer reads graphs through one interface: open the space (each graph with its role, ontology, producing definition and inputs), take a snapshot of a graph, subscribe to it (patches, rejections and an end, in order), and optionally read its measurements, follow lineage, and evaluate a definition. Any program that offers this interface can feed the viewer, the engine or a set of files the engine wrote.

**Theorem 12.1 (a replica is correct).** A replica that follows Def. 12.2 over a feed holds `G_r` after it has applied the patch of version `r`, for every `r` up to the first rejected patch, and so holds a state equal to the engine's at that version. A replica of a scoped graph (Def. 3.12) holds the scoped state.

*Proof.* The replica starts at `G_{r_s}`: the snapshot is the state at its version (Theorem 6.4). Each accepted patch passes the guard of Def. 3.7, so applying it gives the next state by Def. 3.8. By Theorem 11.5 the received patches are the log in order, so no version is skipped. A rejected patch stops the replica and is visible, so a replica is never silently ahead of or behind the log. ∎

The proof has been read, not checked by a program. That the viewer implements Def. 12.2 is tested: on the viewer's side, the rules for applying and rejecting, against a fake connection; on the engine's side, order, resuming, gaps and a reader ahead of the head. The statement end to end, a replica digest compared with the engine's state digest at every version, has not been checked.

**Proposition 12.2 (joining late).** A reader that joins late reads the past through snapshots and patches, and follows the present through a stream. The two parts agree if following starts at the version after the last patch read.

*Proof.* Theorem 6.4. ∎

**Theorem 12.3 (the same entity across graphs).** Two elements in different graphs of a space with the same entity id denote the same entity, if both ids were produced through one identity domain from equal canonical components. An aggregate is meant to have a new identity and not to reuse the id of a member. Highlighting "the same entity id in every graph" is therefore sound for entities and says nothing about aggregates.

The first sentence is proved: one key gives one id (Theorem 1.4), and a key is bound to the id of another key only by an alias or a merge, both recorded with evidence (Def. 1.10, Theorem 1.7), so the same id means the same entity. Its converse, one entity has one id, holds only through `Rep`: after a merge, a graph that was not evaluated again can still hold the element under the id that did not survive, and the highlight then misses it without being wrong. The second sentence is a rule for authors (Def. 5.9), not a consequence of typing: no rule of Ch. 5 (5.2) refuses an emit whose identity is the id of a member carried through an aggregate.

## 12.2 Measurements on one time axis

A version of a graph and a window of measurements are indexed differently: versions by the log, measurements by observed time `(g, k, p, τ)` with no versions (Ch. 7). One slider that moves "version and measurements together" needs a defined map between them.

**Definition 12.4 (time alignment).** `rev_of(τ)` is the largest version of graph `g` whose logical time is at most `τ`. The *logical time of a version* is the largest advance `λ` among the inputs up to the cause of its commit. The aligned view of an element at slider position `τ` is `view(k, rev_of(τ), [τ − w, τ))` (Def. 7.9).

**Theorem 12.4 (alignment is well defined).** `rev_of` never decreases and is defined for every `τ` at or after the first commit, and the aligned view is a function of `(G, M, τ)`, where `G` is the log of the graph and `M` its measurements.

*Proof.* The commit record of Def. 3.8 does not hold the logical time of a version, so it is read from the input log, or from a field that a store would have to add to the record. It does not decrease along the versions: causes follow the recorded order (F1 of Theorem 3.5), and `λ` only increases within a projection (Ch. 3, 3.4). So the largest version with `λ ≤ τ` exists, and a larger `τ` gives a version at least as large. The view is a function of its arguments (Theorem 7.6). ∎

The prototype does not have `rev_of`. Its versions carry no time, so the viewer keeps one time axis for the whole space, puts measurements at the start of their windows, places versions by their order on the windows, and says so on the timeline.

## 12.3 Definitions in the viewer

**Proposition 12.5 (definitions need no new mechanism).** Definitions are graphs of the catalog (Def. 2.1). A subscriber can therefore follow the catalog graph and its modules exactly like a data graph, and Theorem 12.1 applies. A change of a definition is a definition edit (Ch. 9), proposed as a patch on the catalog, on a draft (Ch. 4, 4.6).

*Proof.* Def. 2.1 and Theorem 12.1. ∎

The engine serves the catalog like any data graph, with the kernel as its ontology, with path ids as element ids (Def. 2.3) and relationship ids `<source id>|<edge type>|<target id>`. The viewer reads modules, types, identity domains, projections, operators, ports, wires and rules with their class, and edits them through drafts.

**What the editor offers comes from the engine.** The engine describes, as data derived from the kernel, every operator kind, every slot of a declaration and every kind of value; it marks the operators it cannot evaluate, with the reason. The editor builds its lists from that description and needs to know only six names of connecting relationships. Wiring is checked in the viewer and again by the engine on every patch, with messages that name the element of the definition. Text and graph stay in step both ways: an edit of the text is checked by the engine, and the difference between the draft's graph and the parsed text becomes one patch, one step of undo (Ch. 4, 4.6).

**Placeholders.** A new operator taken from the palette comes as a *skeleton*: the operator with one placeholder child in every required slot, so that it can be printed at once (an empty condition, a count `n = count()`). The engine lists the ids of those placeholders beside the patch, and the editor removes exactly those when the author fills the slot. A list and not a property, because in the graph a placeholder is like a child an author wrote: a marker property would be part of the definition, of its digest and of its text.

**What the viewer is told about types.** Three declarations of Ch. 2 exist for people and viewers: value roles (Def. 2.13), the kind of a relationship type and whether it is a hierarchy (Def. 2.14), and the label of an entity type (Def. 2.15). The engine serves them with the ontology of every graph, and serves the description of each projection (Def. 5.1) with each graph. Nothing in evaluation reads them. A viewer may name an element by its label, lay out a hierarchy as a tree, and choose by kind which edges to show; when a type declares nothing, it falls back to what it did before. The shop's definitions declare labels and kinds for the types a person sees: for example `name` is the label of a pod and `reason` that of a warning; calls are communication, `Backs` and `RunsOn` are composition, `DependsOn` of the health model is dependency, `About` and `Concerns` are support.

**Value roles give colors.** When a graph's ontology has roles for a property, that property colors the elements by default. The roles map to the status colors of the viewer's theme; a value without a role is neutral. Color is never the only sign: the legend lists value, count, role, a glyph and a word; labels carry the value and the glyph; edges of the role danger are dashed; and the list of elements has a column with the value and the role. A patch that changes the property recolors the element, and moving the slider shows the state at that version, counts included. When an element has roles for several properties (the health graph has three), it takes the worst of them, and its text names the property.

*Example:* the database server turns `Unhealthy`. The patch reaches the viewer, its node becomes red with the glyph ✖ and the word, and so do the nodes of what depends on it; the legend's count of `Unhealthy` goes up by six.

**Levels in the viewer.** A level is useful only to a reader who can see what it abstracts (Ch. 5, 5.8). Four behaviors serve that, all read from what the engine serves and none from names.
- *Highlight across levels.* Selecting an element highlights, in every graph, what it stands for below and what it is part of above, following lineage (Ch. 9, 9.5) level by level down to the master and back up. Checked on the synthetic estate: selecting a lifted capability edge highlights 3 elements in the service level and 9 in the master. The highlight is as complete as the lineage.
- *Stubs for references.* A relationship whose end the graph does not hold (Def. 3.14) is drawn to a hollow, dashed stub, labelled from the identity key the engine reports, and the viewer says why the end is missing: absent, or held in which other graph. On the served newest recording, in runs of the project that the authors did not repeat (Ch. 14, 14.1), the master graph showed 589 stubs (563 Azure resources, 26 pods) and no edge left undrawn. At most 5,000 stubs are drawn per graph.
- *Supporting elements fold away.* In a graph with value roles, elements that only support others are folded onto the node they support, as a count that opens. Which elements those are is decided from the ontology alone: a relationship type whose one end has no property with roles and whose other end has. In the same runs, the health graph of the earlier health model, since replaced by the workload model (Ch. 7, 7.4), showed 11 components and 12 signals, 143 evidence elements as counts, and a failure propagating through it.
- *A graph without edges says so*, when its ontology declares relationship types, so that missing lines are not taken for a fault of drawing.

**The default content is engine output.** The viewer opens on the files that the engine writes for the synthetic estate; nothing in them is written by hand. They show the master graph with 47 nodes and 48 edges beside each projection: the service level 4 and 5, capabilities 4 and 4, deployment health 3 and 2, health 40 and 45. The authors generated them again with the engine and found them equal byte for byte to the files in the project. These counts are of the earlier health model; the default content and the walkthroughs now use the workload model, and the authors have not run them since.

**Every operator kind can be built without text.** A *walkthrough* is a list of editor commands sent to a draft on the engine. A script plays each walkthrough in a browser against the engine and checks: every command is accepted; the engine reports no problem beyond those the file allows; the projection the commands rebuild prints the same canonical text as the original and previews with the same digest; the same projection written as text and applied through the text path prints and previews the same; and, where the file asks, the preview has elements. Two walkthroughs build projections from nothing (deployment health, 39 lines; the health roll-up, 59 lines) and print the same text as the originals.

*Results,* the authors' runs of eight walkthroughs:

| Data | Passed | Elements in the previews (entities and relationships) |
|---|---|---|
| synthetic estate, strict | 8 of 8 (112, 32, 180, 10, 19, 60, 30 and 21 commands) | deployment health 3 and 2; a `Fixpoint` closure 0 and 26; health 40 and 45, its text twin the same; `Retain` 2; `Series`, `Window` and `Threshold` 4, its text twin 4; `Union` 10; `Values` 2; `Link` 0 and 0 |
| newest real recording, empty previews allowed | 8 of 8 | deployment health 22 and 36; the closure 0 and 33; health 347 and 353; `Retain` 91; the series projection 3; `Union` 11; `Values` 2 |

A `Link` emits no element, so its walkthrough checks the effect on identity that a preview reports: on the estate 5 more ids created and 5 more merged than in the live space, none aliased, no conflict. On the real recording the effect is not comparable, because the preview saw part of the input. The health counts of the real recording were taken before two later fixes and are superseded by the figures of Ch. 7. Two more walkthroughs rebuild the two levels with `lift` (82 and 67 commands, Ch. 5, 5.8), both equal to the originals; with them, ten walkthroughs pass on the estate, in runs of the project that the authors did not repeat.

**A projection built through the editor alone.** Against the engine on the newest recording (66 pods, 15 services), a projection was built with the same commands the viewer sends: over the master graph, with a parameter `minReady` bound to 1, a `Match`, a `Group` that sums ready pods per service, and an `EmitEntity` of a health component with `state = If(ready > minReady, "Healthy", "Unhealthy")`. The engine printed the module without a problem, a preview over part of the input (500,824 of 579,175 rows; the report does not say which `n` the run used, and 500,824 is not the default of Def. 9.11) gave 13 components in 350 ms (7 healthy, 6 unhealthy), and the commit created the graph with 13 nodes. The engine's checker taught the editor four rules on the way: an optional property in a comparison needs `coalesce`; a property read after a group must be a key of the group; an emit that creates must set every required property; a new `Group` comes with placeholders to delete. The developers of the viewer report this run; the authors did not repeat it.

**Refactoring from the viewer.** A person selects operators or rules and moves them into a new projection, inlines a projection, or moves a rule (Ch. 5, 5.7). The engine plans the patch, the viewer shows its size first, and applying it adds one step of undo to the draft. The engine's refusals are shown as they come. That the outputs stay equal (Proposition 5.7) is checked by the engine's tests, not by the viewer.

**Access without a mouse or without color.** A list of elements, with filters, shows the same graph as a table, and the inspector lists connections as a tree that is reached with the keyboard. This is the alternative to the drawn graph, and the only view when the browser cannot draw it. The authors' run of the checking script in Chrome on the synthetic estate found no violation in 32 checks of pages and dialogs by a standard checker of accessibility, passed all 12 checks of keyboard and highlight, and found every role color of the legend in the three themes (light, dark, high contrast). **Not done:** a pass with a screen reader; browsers other than Chrome. The drawn graph is one focusable group whose content is reached through the list and the inspector, and dragging in the editor has keyboard equivalents only for moving, connecting and selecting a region.

## 12.4 One ordered stream of events

**Definition 12.7 (ordered stream of events).** The engine holds the committed state and emits one stream of events, each with a sequence number `seq` for the whole server that increases by one: snapshots, patches, measurements, activations, changes of drafts, ends and errors. A subscription names graphs and may give `after = seq`. Without `after` it receives one snapshot per graph, current as of its `seq`, and then every later event. With `after` it receives the later events; if `after` is older than the events the engine still keeps (the last 20,000 by default, Ch. 11, 11.6), it receives `gap` and then snapshots. A reader ahead of the server is told so. A subscription with more than 10,000 messages waiting is closed.

**Proposition 12.9 (the rule of a client).** A client that applies a patch of graph `g` iff the patch starts at its own head of `g`, and otherwise subscribes again with `after` equal to the last `seq` it applied, holds for every graph a state equal to the engine's state at some version, and never skips or repeats a version, except across a reported gap, after which it holds the version of the snapshot it receives.

*Proof.* A client that applies a patch only at its own head is a replica in the sense of Def. 12.2 for each graph (Theorem 12.1). A new subscription with `after` starts the feed at the next event, which Theorem 11.5 makes free of gaps or reports as `gap`. ∎

The proof has been read, not checked by a program. Tested: order, resuming, gaps and a reader ahead of the head, on the engine; reconnecting, resuming after the last sequence number and dropping duplicates by version (patches) and by window time (measurements), on the viewer against a fake connection; and one live run in which the engine was killed and started again, reported by the viewer's developers. The messages for a gap, a reader ahead, a slow reader and an activation are tested against a fake connection only. Inside the viewer, patches and measurements never redraw the page themselves: they produce summaries at most every 100 ms (data) or 250 ms (time), so a stream of patches does not overload the page.

**Proposition 12.10 (resources, tags and caching).** (a) Within one run of one store, an answer addressed by a version (a graph at version `r`, the patches between two versions not above the head, a document at version `r`) is a function of its address, and an answer addressed by a digest (a release, its text, its difference) is a function of the digest. (b) A version number, a catalog version and a sequence number are unique only within one run of one store: two runs with different definitions reach the same catalog version, and two runs can have the same graph version with different content. Therefore only answers whose address contains a content digest are marked as never changing. Every other answer that may be cached must be checked again. And **every** *tag* of an answer, the short name a browser uses to ask "has this changed?", is the first 32 hexadecimal digits of SHA-256 over the media type, a newline and the bytes of the body, and nothing else. Equal bytes have equal tags in any run and after a restart from a store, different bytes never share a tag unless `H` has a collision, a tag the server does not know is answered with the body and the current tag, and a matching tag is answered with "not modified".

*Proof of (a).* The past does not change (Theorem 3.2), and releases are named by their content (Theorem 2.4). ∎ Part (b) defines the engine's behavior. Its consequences are tested: two engines whose definitions differ in one value role give different tags for the ontology at the same catalog version; the same content has the same tag after a restart and in another engine; a stale tag is answered with the body, and a matching one with "not modified" without building the body. The rule replaced an earlier one, found in runs of the project, under which tags were built from a graph name and a version. After definitions changed, a browser asked again with its old tag, was told "not modified", and kept an ontology without value roles.

## 12.5 Documents as projections

A *document projection* turns graph facts into an ordered list of blocks of text. The engine has it **without a change of the kernel**: a document is a derived graph whose output ontology is the module of documents, and any projection can produce one.

**Definition 12.5 (document, block, patch of a document).** A document is `D = (order, blocks)`, with `order` a list of block ids without repetition and `blocks` a map from block id to content (a heading, a paragraph, a table with keyed rows, a list, code). A document has its own version, the versions of the graphs it was made from, the version of its projection, and a flag `Complete` or `ExplicitlyTruncated`. A patch of a document is `P = (E, order_before, order_after, sources_before, sources_after)`, with `E` a set of edits of blocks `(id, before, after)` with distinct ids. Orders and edits are independent.

The id of a block is derived from the rule and the identity or group key of what it shows, never from its position or its text: `bid = base32(H(enc([rule, group key])))`. In the engine the id is allocated through the identity domain `block(rule, key)` (Def. 12.8), which gives the same function of rule and key. A row of a table has a key from the fields that identify it. The order of rows must be total, so a template appends a stable identity as the last key.

**Definition 12.8 (document graph, as built).** The ontology of documents has one entity type `Block { kind, order, text?, level?, a?, b?, c?, d?, n? }` and the identity domain `block(rule, key)`, so the id of a block is derived from the rule and the key. `kind` is `heading` (with `level` 1 to 6), `paragraph`, `item`, `stat` (`text` is the label, `n` the value), `header` or `row` (cells `a` to `d`, or `n` instead of `d`); consecutive `header` and `row` blocks form one table. `order` is a text to sort by, and blocks are ordered by `order` and then by id, so the order is total. The engine renders a document graph at the head or at a version, as Markdown or as JSON.

**How the built form differs from Def. 12.5.** The document is the graph, so its version is the graph's version, and a patch of the document is a patch of the graph: its inverse is Theorem 4.3, not the separate statement of Theorem 12.7. `order` is a property of each block, not a list beside the blocks. The versions of the source graphs are not part of the rendered document. The flag `ExplicitlyTruncated` is not built, so a document over a graph without bound has no declared truncation and renders in full.

**Theorem 12.6 (stable block identity).** Block ids do not change when the output is reordered, when a label changes or when a row moves. Two blocks have the same id iff the rule and the group key are equal, unless `H` has a collision.

*Proof.* `bid` is a function of the canonical bytes of `[rule, key]` and of nothing else (Theorem 1.1). ∎ The identity domain `block(rule, key)` of the engine has exactly these components.

**Theorem 12.7 (inverting a patch of a document).** If `apply(P, D)` is defined, then `apply(inverse(P), apply(P, D)) = D`, where `inverse(P)` swaps before and after in every edit, in the orders and in the source versions, and swaps the versions. A patch applied to a document at another version, order or content is rejected.

*Proof.* Edits are keyed by distinct block ids and orders are separate lists, so `apply` is the guarded edit of Def. 3.7 on the map `blocks` together with a replacement of the list `order`, whose guards are the exact base. The inverse has the matching guards on the result, so the argument of Theorem 4.3 applies to each of the two parts. A different base fails a guard. ∎ The theorem is about the model of Def. 12.5; in the engine it is Theorem 4.3 for graph patches.

**Theorem 12.8 (rendering is a function; escaping).** A renderer maps a document at one version to bytes, and is a function of that document and of the pinned version of the renderer. Text from graph data is escaped, so that it cannot create structure in the document.

That rendering is a function holds by definition and is tested. Escaping is tested on four examples and is not a proved property. The renderer writes one kind of line end and one final newline, puts a backslash before each character that has a meaning in Markdown, and turns a line break into a space. Tests: four cases (plain text, the separator of table cells, the syntax of a heading and of a link, a line break); blocks come out in the order of `order` and then id, whatever the order in which the graph was built; and a document is the same bytes at every version in two runs over the same input. The theorem counts as tested.

**Proposition 12.11 (one digest of a document per version).** The digest of a document at version `r` is the SHA-256 of its rendered Markdown. Equal graph states give equal documents and equal digests, and the digest changes when a rendered block changes. The tag of the answer is that of Proposition 12.10, so it differs between formats and is equal after a restart.

*Proof.* Theorem 12.8, first sentence. ∎ It is tested on examples and measured.
- On the fixture of deployment health (an invented shop), the report has two rows at the head. Earlier versions show the deployment `db` unhealthy with 0 ready pods at version 5, `web` with 2 ready pods at version 3, and an empty table at version 1, and the digests differ per version.
- On the 95-minute recording the report has 26 blocks (one heading, two numbers, one table header, 22 rows) and the same digest when the input is cut into batches of 1, where the graph is at version 60, and into batches of 100,000, where it is at version 39. The document is a function of the graph state, not of the version numbers. Its text is not kept, because it names workloads of the real cluster.

**Worked example.** The shop's report of deployments reads the deployment health graph. It writes a heading; two numbers, the deployments and the unhealthy ones, by `Group`; a table header; and one row per deployment, keyed by cluster, namespace and name. It is ordinary definition text: `Values` for the title and the header, `Group` for the totals, `EmitEntity` of `Block` with identity `block(rule, key)`. Nothing in the engine knows the report; the engine knows the ontology of documents and one renderer. A renderer for another kind of block or another format is a new release of the program.

**The document in the viewer.** A graph that is a document shows as a page, with a second tab for the graph. The page is the Markdown of the version that the timeline shows, so moving the slider changes the document; at the head it follows the stream and asks again with its tag. Tests: the example report parses to blocks; a version is fetched once and then kept; the head is checked again and kept when not modified; the view follows the timeline and a patch; a failing engine gives a message; no accessibility violation is found in three themes. *Following the live stream:* against an engine that plays the fixtures at twice their speed, with the page opened once and never reloaded, the report passes through versions 2 and 3 and ends at version 3 with the digest that the engine gives. *Not tested:* whether the digest the page shows at a version equals the one the engine reports.

## 12.6 What the prototype does differently

- **No `rev_of`.** Versions carry no time (12.2).
- **No edits of data in the viewer.** The engine accepts edits of views (Ch. 8); the viewer has no way to make them.
- **A replica digest is not compared** with the engine's at each version (Theorem 12.1).
- **Documents** have no truncation; a document over a large graph renders in full.
- **What the viewer reads of newer engine features** (references served alone, named stubs, measurements in the default content) was read in the program by the authors and not checked in a browser. In the default content a document is the head only, and measurements arrive all at once after the history, not with the patches.
- **The color chosen per graph** is kept in the browser, not on the engine.
- **Tests.** The authors ran 643 tests of the viewer, a clean type check, the walkthroughs (8 of 8) and the accessibility script, against two states of the engine with the same results. Runs of the project that the authors did not repeat report 672 tests and 10 of 10 walkthroughs at a later state of the viewer.
